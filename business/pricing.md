# Pricing & Terms

## Why not cheaper

Beginner short-form rates run **$50–250 per clip**. At $250 for five you're already at the bottom
of the market — that's deliberate while you have no reviews, and it's low enough that you should
never go under it.

Charging $50 doesn't win price-sensitive clients, it *selects* for them. The client who haggles
from $250 to $100 is reliably the one who wants six revision rounds, sends footage three days
late, and disputes the invoice. The cheapest work is the most expensive to deliver.

Raise prices after every 3 completed jobs until people start saying no. When nobody says no,
you're too cheap.

## Terms — put these in writing before you start

- **50% deposit up front**, balance on delivery. Non-negotiable for direct clients. It filters
  people who were never going to pay, and it means you can never be fully stiffed.
- **One revision round included.** Additional rounds $50. Say this *before* the first delivery,
  not after they ask for a fourth.
- **Turnaround starts when the footage lands**, not when they pay. Otherwise you're on the hook
  for a deadline while waiting on a file.
- **They own the clips on final payment.** Simple, and it's what they expect.
- **You may show the work in your portfolio** unless they ask you not to. Ask once, up front —
  this is how you build samples you're actually allowed to use.

## Getting paid

You have Zelle and CashApp. That works for individual creators, who pay freelancers this way all
the time.

**Add PayPal invoicing anyway.** An invoice with line items reads as a business; a Zelle request
to a phone number reads as a stranger. It costs you nothing to have both and offer whichever the
client prefers. For anyone paying from a company account, the invoice is often the only thing
their bookkeeping will accept.

Two things worth knowing about P2P apps: they carry essentially no recourse if a job goes wrong,
and personal accounts aren't really intended for business income. Neither is a reason to avoid
them for small creator jobs — just don't make them your only option, and keep your own record of
every payment for taxes.

## Tax note

Freelance income is taxable and nobody withholds it for you. Set aside roughly **25–30%** of
everything you earn from the first dollar, and keep the receipts for software and hardware —
they're deductible. Getting a surprise bill next April is the most common way a first freelance
year ends badly. If your income gets substantial, talk to an actual accountant; I'm not one.

## Quoting rule

State the price in the first message. Every "let's get on a call to discuss pricing" costs you a
day and loses the buyers who'd have just said yes.
